Mental health care for children: a guide for parents and teachers

When restlessness is more than restlessness, what a first assessment involves, and how school can help.

Read the article

Every child has difficult weeks. A bad exam, a fight with a friend, a new school. What parents and teachers need to notice is change that lasts: more than a few weeks, in more than one place, and getting in the way of learning, friendships or sleep.

Signs worth a conversation

A child who cannot sit through a meal or a lesson, who loses things every day and forgets instructions. A child who was friendly and now sits alone at recess. Stomach aches or headaches every school morning with nothing found by the paediatrician. A sudden drop in marks. Fear of being away from the mother that has not settled by Class 3. Speech or eye contact that is behind other children of the same age. Anger that ends in broken things. A teenager who has stopped eating or sleeping, or who talks about being a burden.

What a first visit involves

The first visit is 30 to 40 minutes. We talk to the parents and to the child, separately and together. We ask for the school's observations, so bring a note from the class teacher or a filled attention checklist from our self-check page. We look at hearing, sleep, screen time and the family's own history, because most problems have more than one cause. Nobody is labelled on day one.

What treatment looks like

For most children the plan starts at home and at school: routines, short clear instructions, movement breaks, no screen in the hour before bed, and a teacher who knows what to look for. Some conditions, such as ADHD or severe anxiety, improve further with medicine. If medicine is suggested you will hear why, for how long, what to watch for, and how we will measure whether it is working. Nothing starts without your agreement.

For teachers

You see thirty children of the same age every day, so you usually notice first. Write down what you see, when it happens and what helps. That one page saves the family weeks. The earlier a child is understood, the less they fall behind.

Sexual problems: symptoms, causes and the treatment nobody talks about

Most sexual difficulties are common, medical and treatable. What a private consult looks like.

Read the article

Most men who come to our sexual health clinic have waited two to five years. Many spent that time on advertisements, powders and advice from friends. Almost all of them could have been helped much earlier.

The common problems

Erectile dysfunction: the erection is not firm enough, or does not last, in most attempts over a few months. Premature ejaculation: ejaculation within about a minute of starting, with little control and distress for the man or the couple. Low desire: little interest in sex for months, often from low mood, stress, medicines, hormones or strain in the relationship. Performance anxiety: the mind watches the body so closely that the body cannot respond; it often begins after one bad night and then repeats. Women's concerns are seen too: pain, low desire and difficulty with arousal.

The "Dhat" worry

Many young men in Gujarat worry that semen is being lost in urine, in night emissions or through masturbation, and that this is causing weakness, dullness and thinning. Semen loss does not weaken the body. The tiredness and anxiety are real and treatable; the belief behind them is not a disease of the body. Explaining this properly is often half the treatment.

Why it happens

Diabetes, blood pressure, thyroid problems, smoking, gutka and alcohol, some medicines, depression, anxiety and relationship strain are the usual causes, often two or three together. That is why a psychiatrist who also runs a sexual health clinic asks about your sugar levels and your sleep in the same visit.

What is treatable

Nearly all of it. Treatment can include medicines, behavioural techniques for the couple, treating the illness underneath, and short counselling. Many couples notice change within weeks.

What a private consult looks like

Book with an alias and a phone number. Choose the first or last slot of a session. Reception asks nothing further. You meet the doctor alone, or with your partner if you prefer. Nothing is written on an outside file. Video follow-ups are available if you live outside Bhavnagar.

Tobacco and gutka: why quitting fails, and what works instead

Nicotine, the first 72 hours, medicines that reduce craving, and the role of the family.

Read the article

In Saurashtra, tobacco rarely means cigarettes. It means gutka, mawa, bidi and chewing tobacco, often from the age of fifteen, often ten or twenty packets a day. If you have tried to quit and failed, you are not weak. You are up against nicotine.

Why quitting fails

Nicotine reaches the brain in seconds and trains it to expect a hit every half hour. When the hits stop, the brain protests: restlessness, irritability, poor sleep, trouble concentrating, a hollow feeling in the stomach, and a craving that feels like hunger. Gutka adds something else. The habit is tied to tea, to the shop, to after meals, to driving, to friends. Willpower alone has to fight the chemistry and the routine together, and usually loses by day three.

The first 72 hours

Withdrawal peaks in the first three days and eases over two to four weeks. A single craving lasts three to five minutes whether you give in or not. Plan for it: water, sauf or elaichi, a short walk, a phone call, the craving SOS on your phone. Remove every packet from the house, the scooter and the shop drawer the night before.

What works instead

Medicines that reduce craving and withdrawal exist: nicotine replacement as gum or patch, and prescription tablets that act on the brain's nicotine receptors. With a plan and follow-up, they roughly double the chance of staying quit compared with willpower alone. Your doctor decides which one fits you and for how long. Do not buy them over the counter on a friend's advice.

The family's role

A spouse who nags makes it harder. A spouse who clears gutka from the house, keeps the first week's tea times busy, and does not say "one is fine" at a wedding makes it easier. Our family pledge puts that in writing.

One more thing

Ask us to look inside your mouth. White or red patches, or a mouth that opens less than three fingers wide, need an early check.

Sleep hygiene for shift workers and students

Read the article

A fixed wake-up time matters more than a fixed bedtime. The body sets its clock from the morning, so keep the alarm the same on Sundays too.

Students: no all-nighters before an exam; what you learn after midnight is lost by morning. Keep naps to twenty minutes before 4 pm. No tea, coffee or cold drinks after 4 pm. The phone charges outside the bedroom. Study at the desk, sleep in the bed, never the other way round.

Shift workers: wear sunglasses on the ride home after a night shift, so the morning light does not wake the brain. Darken the room with thick curtains and tell the family the sleeping hours. Keep one "anchor" sleep of at least four hours at the same clock time on work days and off days.

If you lie awake more than three nights a week for three months, that is insomnia, not a habit. Take the ISI sleep check and bring the score to the clinic.

Exam stress in Class 10 and 12

Read the article

Some stress before the boards is useful. It keeps a student at the desk. Too much stress does the opposite: the mind goes blank, sleep breaks, the stomach hurts every morning, and the child says "I cannot do this" every day.

Parents, three things help more than any tuition. Protect seven hours of sleep; a tired brain does not remember. Keep study blocks short, forty minutes with a ten-minute walk, and keep meals at the usual time. Stop comparing with the cousin or the neighbour's child. Praise effort, not marks.

Watch for panic attacks, refusing to go to school, not eating, or any talk of not wanting to live. Take that seriously the first time, not the third. Call us or Tele-MANAS 14416 the same day.

Short treatment in the weeks before an exam is common and works. Nobody has to suffer through March.

Memory & elderly clinic1 min read

Caring for a parent with dementia

Read the article

Forgetting a name is ageing. Forgetting the road home, repeating the same question within minutes, or accusing family of stealing is not. Get it assessed early. Some causes, such as thyroid problems, vitamin deficiency and late-life depression, are treatable and look exactly like dementia.

At home: keep the same routine every day. One place for keys, spectacles and medicines. Labels on cupboards. A name and phone number in the pocket, always. Lock the gas when nobody is in the kitchen. Evening confusion is common; keep the lights on and the house quiet after sunset.

Do not argue with a wrong memory. Correcting your father ten times a day exhausts you both. Agree, and gently change the subject.

Caregivers burn out. Take a break every week and accept help. Ask about an Advance Directive while your parent can still decide; see our rights page.

Fact

Myth

Only weak people get depression.

Tap to flip
Fact

Depression is a medical illness that changes sleep, appetite, energy and thinking. It affects farmers, teachers, doctors and soldiers alike. Asking for help is the strong move.

Myth

ECT is torture. They give shocks to punish people.

Tap to flip
Fact

Modern ECT is given under anaesthesia with a muscle relaxant, while a team monitors you. Myth

Children do not get depression. They are just moody.

Tap to flip
Fact

Children and teenagers do get depression. It shows as irritability, stomach aches, falling marks and pulling away from friends more than as sadness. It responds well to treatment when it is noticed.

Myth

Tap to flip
Fact

The first packet may be a choice. Dependence changes the brain's reward circuit so that the craving overrides the wish to stop. Relapse is part of the illness, not a moral failure, and it responds to medicine and structured support.

FAQ

The questions we are asked every day.

Honest answers, in the order people usually ask them. If yours is not here, ask Saathi or send us a WhatsApp.

About illness and treatment

Is mental illness treatable?

Yes. Most mental illnesses respond to treatment, and many people recover completely. Depression, anxiety disorders, OCD and panic usually improve within weeks of the right treatment. Long-term conditions such as bipolar disorder and schizophrenia are controlled with medicines and support so that people study, work and raise families. The one thing that reliably makes an illness harder to treat is waiting years before the first visit.

What is the difference between psychiatry and psychology?

A psychiatrist is a medical doctor (MBBS, then MD or DPM in psychiatry) who diagnoses mental illness, orders tests, prescribes medicines and manages the physical side. A psychologist is trained in the science of the mind and behaviour and provides assessment and psychotherapy, but does not prescribe. The two often work together on the same patient.

How long will I need medicines?

It depends on the illness, how many episodes you have had, and how fully you recover. For a first episode of depression or anxiety, medicines are usually continued for some months after you feel well, because stopping early is the commonest reason people relapse. For illnesses that return, the course is longer. Your doctor sets the plan, reviews it at every visit, and when the time comes, reduces the dose step by step. Do not stop on your own because you feel fine; feeling fine is the medicine working.

Do psychiatric medicines cause dependence?

The commonly used antidepressants, antipsychotics and mood stabilisers do not cause addiction. You do not crave them and you do not need more and more. Some sleeping tablets and anxiety tablets of the benzodiazepine family can cause dependence if taken daily for long, which is why we prescribe them for short periods, in small quantities, and tell you so. Any medicine should be stopped under guidance, since a sudden stop can bring withdrawal effects or the illness back.

Is stigma a real problem?

Yes, and it costs lives. Fear of what neighbours, in-laws or an employer will say keeps people in Bhavnagar away from treatment for years, and keeps young women from being brought in at all. That is why this site has a Quick exit button, why you can book with an alias, and why nobody at reception asks why you have come. The illness is the problem. Coming here is not.

What is schizophrenia? What causes it and what are the complications?

Schizophrenia is a long-term illness of the brain in which a person may hear voices nobody else hears, hold firm beliefs that are not true (such as being watched or poisoned), speak in a confused way, or withdraw from everyone. It usually begins between 18 and 30. The cause is a mix of genetics and changes in brain chemistry, sometimes triggered by stress or by ganja. Untreated, the complications are serious: loss of studies and work, broken relationships, neglect of health, and a higher risk of suicide. Treated early and consistently, most people live at home, and many work. Family support and not stopping medicines are the two biggest factors.

Counselling vs psychotherapy: what is the difference?

Counselling is shorter and focused on a present problem: a decision, a loss, a conflict, exam stress. Psychotherapy is structured treatment over weeks or months, using a specific method such as cognitive behavioural therapy, to change patterns of thinking and behaviour behind an illness like depression, OCD or panic. Both are talk-based, both are confidential, and both can run alongside medicine.

How are psychiatric illnesses treated?

With a plan, not a single pill. Depending on the illness it may include medicines, psychotherapy or counselling, changes to sleep and daily routine, treating a physical illness underneath, involving the family, and measuring progress with simple scales at each visit. A few severe conditions need hospital care or ECT. You are told what each part is for and how long it will take.

About your visit and this clinic

How do I make my visit useful?

Before you come, write down your three or four main problems with an example of each: "I have not slept more than four hours since Diwali", "I checked the lock eleven times yesterday". Note when it started and what you have tried. Bring every past prescription and report, arranged by date, in one file. Bring the medicines you take now, including Ayurvedic and homoeopathic ones. If a family member saw what happened, bring them. First visits are 30 to 40 minutes; follow-ups 10 to 15.

Why do psychiatric illnesses occur?

Three things add up. Biological: genes, brain chemistry, hormones, a head injury, a physical illness, alcohol or ganja. Psychological: how a person thinks and copes, early losses, trauma. Social: money trouble, a bad marriage, unemployment, loneliness, migration. Doctors call this the biopsychosocial model. It is why the same stress breaks one person and not another, and why treatment looks at all three.

Can meditation and yoga help?

Yes, as part of a plan. Regular meditation, pranayama and exercise reduce anxiety, improve sleep and help prevent relapse of depression. They are not a replacement for treatment in a moderate or severe illness, and we will tell you honestly when something needs more than meditation. Keep practising, and tell your doctor what you do.

How common are personality disorders?

More common than people think. Studies in India and worldwide suggest a few per cent of adults meet the criteria for some personality disorder, though most never get a diagnosis. These are long-standing patterns of relating, reacting and coping that cause repeated trouble at home or work. They are not "bad character". Structured psychotherapy helps, and treating the depression or anxiety that often comes with them helps a lot.

Is a video consultation safe and private?

Video follow-ups run on an encrypted link sent to your phone. Nothing is recorded. Only the doctor is on the call, from a closed room. We ask you to do the same: a closed door and earphones. First visits are better in person; video works well for follow-ups and for people outside Bhavnagar.

Can my family be told about my treatment?

Only if you want them to be. Under the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 your information is confidential. You decide who sits in the room and who gets a copy of the plan. Many patients choose to give a caregiver access to the medicine schedule and appointments through Family Circle, while therapy notes stay private. The exception is danger to your life or someone else's, when the doctor may share what is needed to keep you safe.

Do you keep my data?

We keep what the law and your care need: your clinical record, prescriptions and reports, for as long as medical rules require. We do not sell or share it. You can see it, correct it, and ask for what is no longer needed to be deleted. The full plain-language notice, under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, is on our privacy page.

What is Kiran Saathi?

Saathi is the clinic's AI companion. It talks in Gujarati, Hindi or English, helps you prepare for a visit, explains your own prescription back to you, and guides you through a craving. It never diagnoses, never changes a dose, and anything that sounds unsafe is handed to a human immediately. Everything it drafts is checked and signed by a doctor. Meet Saathi.
